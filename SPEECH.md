# Everything in Time
*Revised graduation speech — Allen School Graduation Celebration, June 12, 2026*

---

A few years ago, at Eastlake High School's graduation, I performed a poem I wrote called "Everything in Time." I opened it with a line I didn't fully understand then:

*"It's in these fleeting moments that I wish I hadn't spent so much time waiting for this time."*

I thought I was just being poetic — something you say at eighteen when endings and beginnings blur together. But I've spent four years learning what that line actually means. And today I want to tell you what I figured out.

I came to the Allen School thinking I understood the deal: work hard, solve problems, keep up. I thought effort would translate cleanly into progress, the way it always had before.

My first year told me otherwise.

I sat in lecture halls surrounded by people who seemed to process everything in real time, while I was still on the first idea. Assignments that looked manageable to others turned into nights where I wasn't just writing code — I was quietly asking whether I was supposed to be here at all.

If you've felt that, I want you to know: you weren't alone. Half the people around you were asking the same question. They just weren't saying it out loud either.

So I did what felt safe. I started living slightly ahead of myself — waiting for the version of college where I'd finally feel confident, waiting to finally catch up. But that moment never came, because I was always waiting for it instead of being in it.

The turning point came from an unexpected place. I found a program called Buildspace, and for the first time I was building something not for a grade, not for a deadline — just because I cared enough to keep going. I stayed up late on something that didn't matter for my GPA and mattered everything for my sense of what was possible. And I wasn't doing it alone. I found people who were also figuring things out in real time.

That community — along with clubs, late-night study sessions, and projects that half-worked — slowly changed how I experienced this place. The Allen School stopped feeling like a place I had to measure up in, and started feeling like a place I could actually contribute to.

And somewhere in there, I started asking different questions.

I'm a CS and Gender Studies student — a combination that raised a few eyebrows, and one I had to defend more than once. But studying both taught me to ask not just *how* to build systems, but *who* those systems are built for, and who they might leave out. That question didn't take me away from computer science. It made me sharper at it. I stopped trying to prove I belonged in this field, and started asking who this field should belong to.

To the families and friends here: I know "computer science" can sound abstract. But what we're leaving with is the ability to build things that will touch real people's lives — how they access healthcare, how they find housing, how their voices are heard or filtered out. The code we write reflects the awareness of the people who wrote it.

So I want to leave all of us — graduates, families, everyone in this room — with this:

Don't disappear from the present while building for the future. Build with attention. Build with care. Keep asking who you might be leaving out.

Because "everything in time" was never about waiting for the right moment to arrive.

It's about realizing — right now, in this stadium, in this imperfect, hard-won, remarkable moment — that you're already in it.

Congratulations, Class of 2026.
