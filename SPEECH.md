# Everything in Time
*Revised graduation speech — Allen School Graduation Celebration, June 12, 2026*

---

A few years ago, in this very stadium at Eastlake High School's graduation, I performed a poem I wrote called "Everything in Time."

It started with a line that I'm still trying to understand:

*"It's in these fleeting moments that I wish I hadn't spent so much time waiting for this time."*

When I came into the Allen School, I thought I understood what it meant to study computer science. Work hard, solve problems, keep up. I thought effort would translate cleanly into progress, just like it had before.

But my first year felt nothing like that.

I sat in lecture halls surrounded by people who seemed to process everything in real time, while I was still trying to catch up to the first idea. Assignments that felt straightforward to others turned into nights where I wasn't just writing code, but quietly questioning whether I was even supposed to be here in the first place.

So I started living slightly ahead of myself. Waiting for the version of college where I would finally feel confident. Waiting for the moment where I would finally feel like I had "caught up."

But that moment never really came, because I was always waiting for it.

That started to change when I found a program called Buildspace that spring.

For the first time, I wasn't building to prove I understood something. I was building just to see what would happen if I stayed with an idea long enough.

And more importantly, I wasn't doing it alone. I found community in other people who were also figuring things out in real time. That experience, along with my time in other clubs, slowly shifted something for me. The Allen School stopped feeling like a place I was trying to measure up in, and started feeling like a place I could actually contribute to.

I started to understand that learning here was never supposed to feel instantaneous. It was supposed to feel lived. Along the way, I found myself thinking about technology in a different way, asking whose experiences are centered, and whose might be missing. And that perspective hasn't taken me away from computer science. It's made me better at it. It changed the question I was asking. I stopped trying to prove I belonged in this field, and started asking who this field should belong to.

As engineers, we spend so much of our time thinking in terms of future impact, what we're going to build, what we're going to fix, what we're going to change. And that's important. But we are leaving here with the ability to build things that will touch people's lives. So I also think we have a responsibility to not disappear from the present while doing it. To build with attention. To build with care. To question what we're optimizing for, and who we might be leaving out when we do.

Because the systems we create don't just reflect our code, they reflect our awareness. And that's what I want to leave with you today, not just as a celebration, though it absolutely is one, but as something to carry.

That poem line — I used to think it was just something I wrote at eighteen, trying to sound meaningful. But I think what it was really trying to say is this: the time you've been waiting for doesn't come later. It's already here. It's been here.

Class of 2026: just remember, you don't arrive at your life. You're already in it.
