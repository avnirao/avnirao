# Everything in Time
*Revised graduation speech — Allen School Graduation Celebration, June 12, 2026*

---

A few years ago, at Eastlake High School's graduation, I performed a poem I wrote called "Everything in Time."

It started with a line I'm still trying to understand:

*"It's in these fleeting moments that I wish I hadn't spent so much time waiting for this time."*

When I came into the Allen School, I thought I understood what it meant to study computer science. Work hard, solve problems, keep up. I thought effort would translate cleanly into progress, just like it had before.

My first year told me otherwise.

I sat in lecture halls surrounded by people who seemed to process everything in real time, while I was still catching up to the first idea. Assignments that felt manageable to others turned into nights where I wasn't just writing code — I was quietly asking whether I was even supposed to be here.

If you've felt that, I want you to know: you weren't alone. Half the people around you were asking the same question. They just weren't saying it out loud.

So I started living slightly ahead of myself — waiting for the version of college where I'd finally feel confident, waiting to finally catch up. But that moment never came, because I was always waiting for it instead of being in it.

That started to change when I found a program called Buildspace. At that point I was still very much in that waiting mindset — waiting to feel ready, waiting to feel like I knew enough. But I applied anyway, with an idea for a project: a way to help undergraduates in tech find resources and community in a more centralized place.

And while I did end up building it, what I found was something else entirely.

For the first time, I wasn't building to prove I understood something. I was building just to see what would happen if I stayed with an idea long enough. I wasn't doing it to pass a class or hit a deadline. I was doing it because I cared enough to keep going. And I wasn't doing it alone — I found people who were also figuring things out in real time, not performing certainty they didn't have.

That community, along with clubs and experiences I found here, slowly shifted something. The Allen School stopped feeling like a place I was trying to measure up in, and started feeling like a place I could actually contribute to.

And somewhere in there, I started asking different questions.

I study CS and Gender Studies — a combination that raised eyebrows more than once, and that I had to defend more than once. But studying both taught me to ask not just *how* to build systems, but *who* those systems are built for, and who they might leave out. That question didn't take me away from computer science. It made me sharper at it. I stopped trying to prove I belonged in this field, and started asking who this field should belong to.

To the families here: I know "computer science" can sound abstract. But what we're leaving with is the ability to build things that touch real people's lives — how they access healthcare, find housing, whether their voices are heard or filtered out. The code we write reflects the awareness of the people who wrote it.

So as we leave here, I want to ask something of all of us — graduates, families, everyone in this room:

Don't disappear from the present while building for the future. Build with attention. Build with care. Keep asking who you might be leaving out.

Because that poem line — *"I wish I hadn't spent so much time waiting for this time"* — it's not about regret.

It's a reminder. The moment you're in is already the one that matters.

Congratulations, Class of 2026.
